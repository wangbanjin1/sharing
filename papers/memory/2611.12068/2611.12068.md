# Introduction

Large language model (LLM) agents now work over horizons far longer than a single context window [@luo2025agentsurvey; @agentmemorysurvey]. A personal agent accumulates months of conversation with its user, and a task agent meets similar problems across many episodes. Memory lets such agents carry information across sessions [@lscs], so that they can personalize their responses [@zhang2026personaagent], stay consistent with what they were told [@memgpt; @mem0], and reuse what worked before [@wang2025awm; @xia2026skillrl]. Benchmarks now measure this ability on long conversations [@locomo; @longmemeval], long documents [@memoryagentbench], multi-session agentic tasks [@he2026memoryarena], and lifelong personalization [@memorycd].

Benchmarks score a memory by its answers, but a team that deploys one asks more of it. A memory that agents can rely on in practice has to meet three requirements at once. It has to be accurate about the particulars of past experience, such as a name, a date, a count, or the distinction between two events, and about the connections between them. It has to be practical, which means deployable with the models a team already uses, nothing to train, answers that can be traced to stored records, and consolidation that cannot destroy what was stored. It also has to be efficient, with answer latency and token cost per question that stay bounded as the history grows.

Each family of existing systems gives up at least one of the three. Systems that consolidate records into extracted facts or abstractions [@mem0; @ye2026autodreamer] are compact but give up accuracy: the details they discard are gone for good, and consolidation can make a memory faulty even when it is distilled from useful experience [@zhang2026faultymemories]. Systems that keep every record and retrieve from them directly lose nothing but give up efficiency, since retrieval must search an ever larger and noisier history [@agentmemorysurvey]. Graph memories organize the same content around entities and their relations [@zep; @edge2024graphrag], but binary triples split an n-ary fact into fragments and lose the connections among its participants [@luo2025hypergraphrag]. Hypergraph memories [@hypermem] group episodes under topics and facts under episodes, but their hyperedges record which unit belongs where rather than the participants of a fact, and retrieval descends from topic summaries written at storage time, so a detail is reachable only after its topic has been retrieved. File-system memories, such as the auto memory of Claude Code [@claudecodememory], route retrieval through summaries in the same way: they keep notes in plain files behind a size-capped index that is loaded into every session, and the agent opens a file on the strength of its one-line summary. A detail that no summary mentions is reachable only by string search, which neither resolves an entity across files nor follows its relations, and index entries past the cap are not loaded at the start of a session. Both families prune top down to bound their cost, and both pay for the pruning with recall on details their summaries do not reflect. Parametric memories store experience in model weights or latent states [@memoryllm; @mplus; @self-param; @behrouz2024titans] and give up practicality, since they require training and rule out closed models.

The requirements also weigh differently for the two uses of memory. Recent studies separate user-centric memory, which personalizes an agent to its user, from agent-centric memory, which accumulates the agent's own experience, and they find that the two face reversed challenges [@agentmemorysurvey; @harnessmemory]. A user-centric memory has to record a user's history broadly and connect its pieces, because a later question may depend on any detail of that history [@memorycd; @ma2026memaudit]. An agent-centric memory has to be precise and refined instead. It distills trajectories into strategies and skills [@zhao2024expel; @ouyang2026reasoningbank; @xia2026metaclaw], and retrieving too much can pull the agent's attention away from the context that matters for its next action [@harnessmemory]. A practical system has to serve both uses from one store. [\[fig:mirix-user-centric,fig:mirix-agent-centric\]](#fig:mirix-user-centric,fig:mirix-agent-centric){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="fig:mirix-user-centric,fig:mirix-agent-centric"} trace one example of each setting through MIRIX-2.0.

In this work, we present MIRIX-2.0, a multi-agent memory system designed to be practical, accurate, and efficient at once. It keeps the multi-agent design of MIRIX [@mirix] and rethinks how memory is stored, indexed, retrieved, and consolidated so that a specific layer meets each requirement ([1](#fig:mirix-overview){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="fig:mirix-overview"}). Six memory agents under a Meta Memory Manager write complete records to a *typed evidence plane* in a vector database, the system of record, so details survive consolidation and every graph fact the answer receives cites the rows it came from. The graph is only an index. The database write commits first, a failed graph write never rolls back a stored memory, and a failed graph read falls back to direct evidence retrieval. A *provenance hypergraph* indexes the entities, events, relations, roles, and times in episodic and semantic records as n-ary frames that cite the rows they were extracted from and carry dates, prices, and counts as literals, and undated events with similar wording keep distinct identities. At query time, a deterministic planner sets explicit budgets, retrieval enters the hypergraph at the anchors that match the question, and the answer receives graph facts together with the records they cite ([2.4](#sec:retrieval){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:retrieval"}). The evidence a question inspects is therefore bounded by construction and does not grow with the length of the history.

Like sleep-time and offline consolidation methods [@lin2025sleeptime; @ye2026autodreamer], AutoDream consolidates memory outside the write path, and it treats the two kinds of memory in opposite ways. For semantic memory, it consolidates identities and never rewrites evidence. It pairs each anchor introduced by new semantic rows with its nearest anchors of the same identity-stable type, such as a person or a place, and asks a language model whether a pair denotes one entity. A confirmed merge unites the citations of the two anchors and rewires the frames that mention them, while event anchors, fact frames, and stored records stay unchanged, so a merge can never destroy a stored memory. Its cost depends on the newly written semantic frontier rather than on the total size of memory, verification runs under explicit pair caps, and negative decisions are cached so that later cycles do not pay for the same pair again. For procedural memory, AutoDream refines instead. It judges each finished task session from quotes in its transcript, distills lessons worth reusing and mistakes worth avoiding, and lets a curator [@zhang2026ace] create or edit skills whose edits can cite the experiences behind them.

We evaluate MIRIX-2.0 on conversational memory, long-document question answering, personalized generation, and procedural memory for interactive agents. MIRIX-2.0 has the highest accuracy of all compared memory systems on both conversational benchmarks, 91.4% on LoCoMo in our runs and 72.6% on LongMemEval-S, 14.3 points above the strongest baseline. On LoCoMo, the hypergraph raises accuracy over flat memory from 85.7% to 88.4%, with the largest gain on multi-hop questions, and AutoDream raises every question type and lifts the overall score to 91.4%. On LongMemEval-S, the hypergraph alone adds 11.2 points. On the single-hop and multi-hop document QA tasks of MemoryAgentBench [@memoryagentbench], with AutoDream disabled, it answers 83.0% and 71.0% of the questions, 31.0 and 22.0 points above the strongest baseline, and on MemoryCD [@memorycd] it has the highest value on all four metrics for cross-domain review summarization. Used as procedural memory, MIRIX-2.0 gives the highest mean success among the compared memory methods on ALFWorld [@shridhar2021alfworld], at 76.4% with GPT-6 Luna against 73.6% for Agent Workflow Memory (AWM) [@wang2025awm] and 69.4% without memory, and on WebShop [@yao2022webshop], at 37.4%. With GPT-5.2 it reaches 49.2% accuracy on MetaClaw-Bench [@xia2026metaclaw], against 44.0% reported for the MetaClaw skill library.

What makes these results usable is the cost at which they come. With AutoDream disabled, MIRIX-2.0 answers faster than HyperMem, the strongest baseline, on all five benchmarks we timed, ingests the two conversational benchmarks in less time, and uses fewer answer tokens on four of the five. As procedural memory, on WebShop it uses the fewest training and test tokens among the memory methods while reaching the highest success. The cost is paid when long documents are ingested. Building the graph makes their ingestion about four times slower than flat memory and slower than that baseline. The full system with AutoDream is not timed, and the retrieval bounds cover the evidence a question inspects, not latency measured as the history grows. No model is fine-tuned, the same system runs with closed and open models, and the code is public.

Our contributions are as follows.

- **Accurate and practical.** A layered memory design in which six typed memory agents keep complete records as the system of record and a provenance-preserving n-ary hypergraph serves only as their index, with occurrence identities that keep distinct events apart and fail-soft indexing that never rolls back a stored memory.

- **One memory for users and agents.** To the best of our knowledge, MIRIX-2.0 is the first memory system that handles both user-centric and agent-centric memory, serving personalization and the refinement of agent skills from one store.

- **Efficient and safe to consolidate.** Graph-owned retrieval runs under explicit budgets and returns graph facts together with the records that justify them. AutoDream merges duplicate entity anchors under explicit caps without touching stored memories, which adds 3.0 points on LoCoMo, and distills task sessions into skills for procedural memory.

# Methodology {#sec:method}

MIRIX-2.0 is designed to be practical, accurate, and efficient. It has to run with the models a team already uses and leave stored records intact, it has to keep a name, a date, a count, or the distinction between two events recoverable after writing and after consolidation, and it has to answer from a bounded evidence set however long the history grows. This section describes the layered design that delivers the three.

MIRIX-2.0 separates storage from indexing ([1](#fig:mirix-overview){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="fig:mirix-overview"}). A *typed evidence plane* stores complete memories and is the system of record ([2.2](#sec:typed-evidence){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:typed-evidence"}). A *provenance hypergraph* indexes the entities, events, relations, roles, and times in those memories, and every fact in it cites the records it came from ([2.3](#sec:hypergraph){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:hypergraph"}). Three paths operate on the two layers, and [\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"} states each of them in pseudocode. The write path stores a memory and then indexes it. The read path plans a query, traverses the graph, and dereferences only the records it selects ([2.4](#sec:retrieval){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:retrieval"}). AutoDream, the third path, runs outside the other two ([2.5](#sec:autodream){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:autodream"}). On semantic memory it merges duplicate anchors in the hypergraph and returns the evidence store unchanged, so semantic consolidation changes how evidence is connected, never what evidence exists. On procedural memory, AutoDream instead distills task sessions into skills that cite the experiences behind them. [6.6](#app:method-invariants){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-invariants"} collects the properties that hold regardless of how the language models behave.

## Problem setup {#sec:method-setup}

For a user $u$, the system observes a stream $\mathcal X_u=(x_1,\ldots,x_T)$ of conversational or multimodal inputs. When it writes $x_t$, future queries are unknown, so the writer must produce a persistent state $$\begin{equation}
    \mathcal S_u^t=(P_u^t,G_u^t)
\end{equation}$$ from which a later query $q$ can be answered with a bounded evidence set $\mathcal R(q;\mathcal S_u^t)$. The evidence store $P_u$ collects the typed stores, among them the episodic store $P_u^E$, the semantic store $P_u^S$, and the procedural store $P_u^P$. We seek high answer utility under three technical requirements.

1.  **Source fidelity.** Concrete evidence remains recoverable after writing and after consolidation.

2.  **Bounded access.** A query inspects a small graph neighborhood and a bounded set of cited rows instead of scanning the full history.

3.  **Temporal identity.** Repeated descriptions of one event may share evidence, but distinct occurrences must not collapse merely because they are worded alike.

Source fidelity and temporal identity are what make the system accurate and its answers traceable, and bounded access is what keeps it efficient. The rest of this section shows how the two layers meet these requirements. Each subsection explains a block of [\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"}, and the numbers on its right point to the equations that define each step.

## Typed evidence plane {#sec:typed-evidence}

A Meta Memory Manager routes each input $x_t$ to the subset $\mathcal K_t=R_\theta(x_t)$ of memory types it updates ([\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"}, line [\[alg:route\]](#alg:route){reference-type="ref" reference="alg:route"}). Each of the six types, Core, Episodic, Semantic, Procedural, Resource, and Knowledge Vault, has its own writer, and the writer of a selected type $k$ produces the record $m_t^{(k)}=W_k(x_t,P_u^{t-1})$ and owns the schema and update operations of that type. Only episodic and semantic records are indexed by the hypergraph. [6.1](#app:method-routing){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-routing"} gives the roles of the six types and the reason for this split.

Each of the six memory types keeps its records in its own schema. The hypergraph indexes the episodic and semantic records, and each of them is represented as $$\begin{equation}
    m=(i,k,y,\tau_e,\tau_m,\rho,\mathbf z),
    \label{eq:memory-record}
\end{equation}$$ where $i$ is an immutable row identifier, $k\in\{\mathrm{episodic},\mathrm{semantic}\}$ is its memory type, $y$ is the stored text, $\tau_e$ is the event time when one applies, $\tau_m$ is the mention or write time, $\rho$ records source and chunk provenance, and $\mathbf z$ holds the search embeddings. Full summaries and details remain in the vector database. The graph stores only identifiers and compact structural metadata.

Writers keep concrete details such as names, counts, and dates in each record, and a record is committed to the vector database before graph construction begins (line [\[alg:commit\]](#alg:commit){reference-type="ref" reference="alg:commit"}), so a failed graph write never rolls back a stored memory ([6.1](#app:method-routing){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-routing"}).

## A provenance-preserving n-ary hypergraph {#sec:hypergraph}

The second half of the write path turns each episodic or semantic record into graph structure ([\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"}, lines [\[alg:extract\]](#alg:extract){reference-type="ref" reference="alg:extract"}--[\[alg:upsert\]](#alg:upsert){reference-type="ref" reference="alg:upsert"}). For each user, MIRIX-2.0 maintains a hypergraph $$\begin{equation}
    G_u=(\mathcal A_u,\mathcal F_u,\mathcal E_u)
\end{equation}$$ with entity or concept anchors $\mathcal A_u$, reified fact frames $\mathcal F_u$, and role-typed incidence edges $\mathcal E_u$. [6.7](#app:method-implementation){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-implementation"} shows the hypergraph MIRIX-2.0 builds for one LoCoMo conversation.

An anchor $$\begin{equation}
    a=(i_a,n_a,t_a,\mathbf e_a,I_a^E,I_a^S)
\end{equation}$$ stores a canonical name $n_a$, an entity type $t_a$, a name embedding $\mathbf e_a$, and the identifiers $I_a^E$ and $I_a^S$ of the episodic and semantic rows that mention it. A deterministic key $\kappa(n)$ normalizes case, punctuation, function words, word order, and singular and plural variants of a name while keeping numbers, and a deterministic admission gate rejects generic filing terms and dialogue-role pseudo-entities and caps the anchors admitted from one record ([6.2](#app:method-graph){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-graph"}).

Surface normalization cannot decide whether two semantically close names denote the same entity. For every extracted entity, approximate nearest-neighbor search therefore retrieves a few existing anchors of the user above a similarity threshold, and one language-model call per record makes a conservative same-entity decision for all of its entities. A rename is accepted only when it selects a retrieved candidate, so embeddings propose comparisons but never authorize a merge on their own. The neighbor count and the threshold, with their relation to the vector-index score, are in [6.2](#app:method-graph){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-graph"}.

For each episodic or semantic record, an extractor produces a set of frames $$\begin{equation}
    f=
    \left(
        p,\,
        \{(r_j,a_j)\}_{j=1}^{d_f},\,
        \ell_f,\,
        \tau_e,\tau_m,\,
        C_f
    \right),
    \label{eq:frame}
\end{equation}$$ where $p$ is a canonical predicate, $r_j$ is the open-vocabulary role of anchor $a_j$, $\ell_f$ maps literal attributes to their values, and $C_f$ is the set of identifiers of the vector-database records the frame cites. A flight, for example, becomes one frame with agent, origin, destination, carrier, and companion arms instead of several disconnected triples, and owns(Mia, Suica card) in [2](#fig:mirix-user-centric){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="fig:mirix-user-centric"} is a frame that cites the May 2 record. The extractor is instructed to keep dates, prices, counts, durations, and measurements as literals on the frame rather than as anchors, so that a value stays attached to the assertion it qualifies. A frame is materialized only if its arguments resolve to at least two distinct anchors of the user. When a deterministic audit finds that the extraction misses high-signal source details, one bounded repair call may add frames that the source supports, and it cannot remove anything the primary extraction produced. [6.2](#app:method-graph){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-graph"} describes the audit.

Semantic claims and episodic occurrences follow different identity rules. Let $\sigma(f)$ be the sorted set of role and anchor keys $\{r_j:\kappa(n_{a_j})\}$. A semantic frame is identified by $$\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{id}_S(f)
    =H(u,p,\sigma(f)),
    \label{eq:semantic-frame-id}
\end{equation}$$ so repeated support for the same durable claim accumulates citations on one node. An episodic frame adds an occurrence key, $$\begin{align}
    \operatorname{id}_E(f)
        &=H(u,p,\sigma(f),\chi(f)), \label{eq:episodic-frame-id}\\
    \chi(f)
        &=
        \begin{cases}
        \text{explicit event time}, & \text{if extracted},\\
        \text{immutable memory id}, & \text{otherwise}.
        \end{cases}
\end{align}$$ Two mentions of the same dated event therefore share one frame, while two undated events with similar wording, such as two runs on different days, stay separate. A frame keeps the time of the event apart from the time it was mentioned, and whether the event is planned or done does not change its identity.

## Graph-owned evidence retrieval {#sec:retrieval}

MIRIX-2.0 treats retrieval as the construction of a bounded evidence set rather than as open-ended similarity search. Episodic and semantic candidates must enter through the graph, and the vector database dereferences and reranks only the identifiers that graph traversal selects. This boundary keeps a hidden full-table search from doing the work attributed to the graph. Only a graph read that raises an error falls back to direct evidence retrieval. A query passes through planning, anchor entry, expansion along two evidence lanes, and fusion, and a verifier then checks the answer ([\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"}, lines [\[alg:plan\]](#alg:plan){reference-type="ref" reference="alg:plan"}--[\[alg:retry\]](#alg:retry){reference-type="ref" reference="alg:retry"}). For the question in [2](#fig:mirix-user-centric){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="fig:mirix-user-centric"}, the planner chooses the single operator, enters the graph at Mia and Tokyo transit, and returns three facts with five rows.

A deterministic planner maps a query to $$\begin{equation}
    \Pi(q)=
    \left(
        o_q,\mathcal P_q,\mathcal C_q,\mathcal Y_q,\mathcal T_q,
        B_q^B,B_q^R
    \right),
    \label{eq:query-plan}
\end{equation}$$ where the operator $o_q$ is single, temporal, intersection, list-union, or count, $\mathcal P_q$ holds canonical relation families and their surface forms, $\mathcal C_q$ holds non-relational constraints, $\mathcal Y_q$ holds expected answer types, and $\mathcal T_q$ holds date and temporal-order constraints. The two budgets cap the base-recall rows and the relation-cited rows, and $B_q^R$ also caps the graph facts that the relation lane renders. The budgets grow with the operator, from 6 base rows and 4 cited rows for a single-value lookup to 8 and 12 for a count, since counting and set construction need broader evidence. [\[tab:lane-budgets\]](#tab:lane-budgets){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="tab:lane-budgets"} in [6.3](#app:method-retrieval){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-retrieval"} lists all five operators.

The query embedding first recalls fifteen anchors by name. A relation query adds the anchors that the question names exactly, and the union is scored by $$\begin{equation}
    s_A(a,q)=s_{\mathrm{vec}}+s_{\mathrm{exact}}+
    s_{\mathrm{lex}}+s_{\mathrm{pred}}+s_{\mathrm{spec}}
    -s_{\mathrm{hub}}.
    \label{eq:anchor-score}
\end{equation}$$ The vector score dominates. Exact mention, token overlap, predicate compatibility, and multi-token specificity break close ties, and a logarithmic degree penalty suppresses generic hubs. The top fifteen anchors are kept, which is the cap $K_A$ of [\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"}. The cap on exactly named anchors and the hub and predicate rules are in [6.3](#app:method-retrieval){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-retrieval"}.

The selected anchors open two bounded lanes. In the base-recall lane, anchors yield the episodic and semantic identifiers directly associated with them, and traversing an incident n-ary frame adds the frame's cited memories and a bounded set of memories associated with its co-participants. In the relation-citation lane, which opens when the query plan contains a relation, the exactly named anchors seed an indexed adjacency lookup over their incident frames. Predicate filtering applies before a cap of 240 frames, the fact score below ranks the rest, and at most $B_q^R$ of them are rendered as explicit graph facts that contribute their exact citations into the vector database. The provenance recomputation of the base lane and the object filter of the relation lane are in [6.3](#app:method-retrieval){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-retrieval"}.

Each relation-lane frame receives the additive score $$\begin{align}
    s_F(f,q)={}&
    s_{\mathrm{rel}}+s_{\mathrm{entity}}+s_{\mathrm{constraint}}
    +s_{\mathrm{type}} \notag\\
    &+s_{\mathrm{time}}+s_{\mathrm{intersection}}
    +s_{\mathrm{specificity}} \notag\\
    &+s_{\mathrm{role}}+s_{\mathrm{state}}.
    \label{eq:fact-score}
\end{align}$$ The first seven terms score relation, named-entity, literal, answer-type, and temporal compatibility, a shared object for intersection questions, and the number of arguments a frame binds. The role term $s_{\mathrm{role}}$ rewards agreement between the argument direction implied by the question and the frame's typed roles, and it penalizes incompatible roles. The state term $s_{\mathrm{state}}$ rewards an event time that matches a requested date and penalizes planned occurrences in count, list-union, and temporal questions. [6.3](#app:method-retrieval){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-retrieval"} describes its fallback and the treatment of intersection questions.

Rows in each lane are reranked, and fusion takes up to $B_q^R$ cited rows, then up to $B_q^B$ base rows, and fills the remaining capacity from the rest of either lane without duplicating row identifiers. The returned evidence therefore contains both the graph facts and the source rows that justify them. The reranking prior, the per-type split, and the rule for near-duplicate paraphrases are also in [6.3](#app:method-retrieval){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-retrieval"}.

In our harness, the answering agent may call the retriever up to five times. After it drafts an answer, a rule-based check requests one more search and one rewrite by the answering model when the draft shows explicit uncertainty, lacks a value the question asks for, or disagrees with the evidence ledger, for example a count without a number or a month that neither the evidence nor the question contains. It also fires on every intersection question (line [\[alg:retry\]](#alg:retry){reference-type="ref" reference="alg:retry"}). The check runs in the answering agent rather than in the memory server, and it never inspects a reference answer. [6.4](#app:method-verification){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-verification"} lists every trigger and the query of the extra search.

## AutoDream: consolidation outside the write path {#sec:autodream}

AutoDream runs outside the critical write path ([\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"}, lines [\[alg:dream-pairs\]](#alg:dream-pairs){reference-type="ref" reference="alg:dream-pairs"}--[\[alg:procedural\]](#alg:procedural){reference-type="ref" reference="alg:procedural"}). It treats the two kinds of memory it consolidates in opposite ways. On semantic memory it is an explicit service call that never schedules itself, and it refines identities under the contract $$\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Dream}(P_u,G_u;\Delta_u)
    = (P_u,\widetilde G_u),
    \label{eq:dream-contract}
\end{equation}$$ where $\Delta_u$ is the newly written semantic frontier and $\widetilde G_u$ is the consolidated hypergraph. The evidence store $P_u$ is returned unchanged. On procedural memory it starts in the background once five task sessions are pending, and it turns them into skills.

Each call receives the exact source-chunk identifiers of the batch just written, which resolve to the newly written semantic rows. These rows define a small frontier $\Delta_u$, so the cost of consolidation depends on new activity rather than on the total size of memory. The invocation schedule of our protocol is in [6.5](#app:method-autodream){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-autodream"}.

Only anchors that are backed by semantic rows and belong to an identity-stable type $t\in\mathcal T_{\mathrm{safe}}=\{\text{person, organization, location,
object, concept}\}$ are eligible. For each eligible anchor $a\in\mathcal A_\Delta$ backed by a frontier row, AutoDream takes the four nearest entries of the name index, which include $a$ itself, and forms the candidate pairs $$\begin{equation}
    \mathcal C_\Delta=
    \left\{
    (a,b):
    \begin{array}{l}
    a\in\mathcal A_\Delta,\;
    b\in\operatorname{NN}_4(a)\setminus\{a\},\\
    \cos(\mathbf e_a,\mathbf e_b)\ge 0.86,\\
    t_a=t_b\in\mathcal T_{\mathrm{safe}}
    \end{array}
    \right\}.
    \label{eq:dream-candidates}
\end{equation}$$ Episodic-only anchors are excluded by construction. An LLM decides whether each pair denotes the same entity and, if it does, chooses one of the two existing names as the canonical representative. Its output is constrained to the submitted pair, and invented representatives are rejected. In [3](#fig:mirix-agent-centric){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="fig:mirix-agent-centric"}, *steel bottle* and *stainless steel bottle* form such a pair.

Every Dream round verifies at most a fixed number of unseen pairs, and the final round also considers the eligible anchors that no earlier round used as a source. Explicit negative decisions are cached as the set $\mathcal N$ of [\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"}, so later cycles do not pay again to reconsider the same pair. [6.5](#app:method-autodream){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-autodream"} states the caps of the intermediate, final, and repair rounds and the cache key.

For a confirmed merge $a\rightarrow b$, the system unions the episodic and semantic row identifiers, keeps aliases, sums mention counts, keeps the maximum admission score, and rewires every role-typed frame arm to $b$. Union, sum, and maximum are commutative and associative, so the order of repeated merges does not change the reachable evidence. The dropped anchor is removed only after its values and edges have been transferred.

Semantic Dream never rewrites the memories in the vector database, and it consolidates neither episodic-only anchors nor fact nodes. The asymmetry is deliberate. Semantic identity can be refined incrementally, whereas event occurrences and their citations are kept as observations. AutoDream therefore makes the graph less fragmented without creating a textual summary whose embedding could hide the details of its inputs.

For procedural memory, AutoDream refines instead of merging. A distiller judges the outcome of each pending session from quotes in its transcript and distills experiences $e_1,\ldots,e_m$, each marked as a lesson worth reusing or a mistake worth avoiding. A curator [@zhang2026ace] turns these experiences into skills in the procedural store $P_u^P$, and it can create, edit, or delete a skill. An edit can cite the experiences behind it with a quote of the text it adds, and a citation is recorded only when that quote checks out, so a cited step of a skill can be traced through its experiences to the quoted sessions they came from. In [3](#fig:mirix-agent-centric){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="fig:mirix-agent-centric"}, one failed purchase yields an experience to avoid and one to reuse, and the curator adds two steps to the shopping routine that cite the first and keeps a step that the second supports. The distiller's unclear verdict and the curator's non-edit verdicts are in [6.5](#app:method-autodream){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-autodream"}.

# Experiments {#sec:experiments}

We evaluate MIRIX-2.0 for the three properties it is designed for. Accuracy is measured for user memory on conversational recall, document QA, and personalized generation ([3.2](#sec:exp-user){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:exp-user"}), which mainly exercise semantic memory together with the episodic records that ground it, and for agent memory on held-out interactive tasks and on MetaClaw-Bench, whose tasks span 30 days ([3.3](#sec:exp-agent){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:exp-agent"}), which exercise procedural memory. Efficiency is measured as ingestion time, answer latency, and answer tokens for user memory, with AutoDream disabled in every timed run, and as training and test tokens for agents ([3.4](#sec:exp-efficiency){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:exp-efficiency"}). Practicality is built into the protocol itself. One system serves both uses, no model is fine-tuned, the agent evaluation covers closed and open backbones, and every graph configuration runs under the budgets of [2.4](#sec:retrieval){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:retrieval"}.

## Experimental setup and system variants {#sec:exp-setup}

Unless a configuration is explicitly identified otherwise, MIRIX-2.0 denotes the full architecture with the provenance hypergraph and AutoDream enabled. For the user-memory ablations, **M0** uses flat vector memory without the graph or AutoDream, like MIRIX-1.0 [@mirix], and the tables label it MIRIX-1.0. **M1** adds graph indexing and retrieval but disables AutoDream, and **M2** is the full graph plus AutoDream configuration. M1 and M2 are configurations of MIRIX-2.0, not successive releases of the method. In M2, semantic AutoDream is invoked by the ingestion driver after every five chunks and after the final chunk, outside the critical write path (Section [2.5](#sec:autodream){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:autodream"} and [6.5](#app:method-autodream){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-autodream"}). It merges eligible entity anchors while preserving stored records and episodic occurrences.

The conversational tables report all three configurations. The available document QA results and graph efficiency measurements use M1 and are labeled accordingly; disabling AutoDream there is an experimental choice, not a restriction that prevents consolidation of document memories. MemoryCD is reported under the system name, but its recorded results do not identify an M0, M1, or M2 configuration, so we do not use them to estimate the contribution of AutoDream. For agent memory, we evaluate the procedural refinement path, in which AutoDream distills sessions into reusable skills whose retrieval is independent of the semantic hypergraph. The M0, M1, and M2 comparisons below therefore concern user-memory indexing and semantic consolidation, rather than a procedural graph ablation.

LoCoMo [@locomo] and LongMemEval-S [@longmemeval] test conversational recall. SHDocQA, MHDocQA, and DetectiveQA in the MemoryAgentBench evaluation [@memoryagentbench] extend this analysis to factual and relational evidence in documents. They are evidence-retrieval tests rather than direct personalization tasks. MemoryCD [@memorycd] tests whether stored user information supports personalized review summarization and generation. We compare with mem0, A-Mem, MemoryBank, Graphiti, and HyperMem on QA, and with the methods listed in the MemoryCD tables, including long-context and RAG baselines, on personalization.

For the user-memory evaluation, the answerer and extractor use `gpt-4.1-mini` with temperature 0 and seed 42, and the topic extractor uses `gpt-4.1-nano`. MIRIX-2.0 uses `text-embedding-3-small`; external baselines retain their default embeddings. SHDocQA and MHDocQA use substring matching, and DetectiveQA uses letter exact matching. LoCoMo and LongMemEval-S use a `gpt-4o-mini` judge with majority-of-3 voting. MemoryCD uses ROUGE-1, ROUGE-L, BLEU-4, and BERTScore-F1. Agent backbones and evaluation protocols are specified in Section [3.3](#sec:exp-agent){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:exp-agent"}. Differences in accuracy or success rate are reported in percentage points.

We report total ingestion wall-clock time, mean time to retrieve and answer a question, total answer tokens, and graph node and edge counts where available. Graph size measures index structure, not stored text length or total storage. For interactive agents, we report success, action steps, and training and test tokens, with mean and standard deviation over three runs. For LoCoMo, all comparisons below use the results in Table [\[tab:locomo\]](#tab:locomo){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:locomo"}; M0, M1, and M2 are distinct system configurations, not repeated runs used to estimate variance.

## Agent memory: held-out tasks and skills {#sec:exp-agent}

We evaluate procedural memory on ALFWorld [@shridhar2021alfworld] and WebShop [@yao2022webshop], comparing an agent without memory with ReasoningBank [@ouyang2026reasoningbank], Agent Workflow Memory (AWM) [@wang2025awm], and MIRIX-2.0. Within each comparison, methods share the actor prompt and backbone. ALFWorld uses GPT-6 Luna and GPT-5.6 Luna; WebShop uses GPT-6 Luna. Each memory method learns from the same training tasks, 39 for ALFWorld and 40 for WebShop. Memory is then frozen for evaluation on 134 held-out ALFWorld episodes with a 50-step cap and 140 WebShop instructions with a 15-step cap. Results are averaged over three independent runs. The separation between memory learning and evaluation tests reuse of acquired procedures on new tasks.

This setting exercises the procedural branch of AutoDream (Section [2.5](#sec:autodream){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:autodream"}). Sessions supply lessons to reuse and mistakes to avoid, and a curator turns those experiences into skills. Unlike semantic consolidation, which preserves the factual records and changes their index, procedural refinement changes the instructions available to the actor. The experiments evaluate the utility of that combined process; they do not separately ablate the outcome judge, experience distiller, or curator.

## Efficiency: ingestion time, answer latency, and tokens {#sec:exp-efficiency}

[4](#fig:res-cost){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="fig:res-cost"}a and [\[tab:eff-docqa\]](#tab:eff-docqa){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="tab:eff-docqa"} in the appendix show the cost of organizing source records before queries arrive. Relative to M0, M1 increases ingestion time by 3.8 to 4.0 times across the three document tasks. M1 also has higher answer latency than M0 on each task, so graph retrieval is not intrinsically cheaper than flat retrieval. Its purpose is to improve access to structured evidence, with extraction and indexing paid in advance and additional evidence handling paid at query time.

Against HyperMem, M1 answers faster on all three document tasks ([4](#fig:res-cost){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="fig:res-cost"}b), at 5.45 versus 10.55 seconds on SHDocQA, 9.01 versus 10.65 on MHDocQA, and 7.24 versus 13.05 on DetectiveQA. It uses fewer answer tokens on SHDocQA and MHDocQA, but more on DetectiveQA, at 1.16M versus 0.35M. The DetectiveQA result shows that lower latency can coexist with higher token use and lower accuracy. Latency, token cost, and answer quality therefore need to be assessed separately.

## What the experiments establish {#sec:exp-discussion}

The results support the three properties in different measure. Accuracy has the broadest support. The M0, M1, and M2 ordering holds on both conversational benchmarks, and MIRIX-2.0 leads the compared systems on four of the five QA benchmarks, two of them with AutoDream disabled. As procedural memory, it has the highest mean success among the compared memory methods on ALFWorld with both backbones and on WebShop, and the highest MetaClaw-Bench score with both backbones, while the no-memory comparisons show that retrieved experience can also hurt. Efficiency holds for answering more than for ingestion. With AutoDream disabled, MIRIX-2.0 answers faster than HyperMem on every timed benchmark, and on WebShop it uses the fewest training and test tokens among the memory methods. Graph construction, however, makes document ingestion 3.8 to 4.0 times slower than flat memory, and the ALFWorld test tokens of MIRIX-2.0 are the highest among the memory methods. Practicality rests on the design and on our protocol rather than on a deployment. One store serves both uses, no model is fine-tuned, and every graph fact returned to the answerer cites its source rows.

The tables evaluate complete configurations, so the component explanations in this section remain interpretations, and the limitations in [5](#sec:conclusion){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:conclusion"} list the ablations that would test them. The tables also do not measure merge precision, source-detail retention, skill provenance coverage, or the contribution of the six-writer routing policy, and they do not report memory length or user-memory ingestion tokens, for which graph counts and answer tokens cannot substitute.

# Related Work {#sec:related}

Memory systems for LLM agents keep past interactions outside the model and retrieve them when they become relevant [@agentmemorysurvey; @zhang2024memorysurvey; @lscs; @guo2026multimodalmemory]. Early designs rank or decay a memory stream [@park2023generative; @MemoryBank] or manage memory the way an operating system manages storage [@memgpt; @memoryos; @li2025memos]. Later systems extract and update salient facts [@mem0], link notes into an evolving network [@amem], compress what they store [@fang2026lightmem; @liu2026simplemem], or learn memory construction with reinforcement learning [@wang2025memalpha; @yan2026memoryr1]. Graph memories organize content around entities and their relations [@bei2025graphsagents; @zep; @gutierrez2024hipporag; @gutierrez2025hipporag2; @edge2024graphrag; @wu2026gam], and hypergraph memories keep n-ary facts together [@luo2025hypergraphrag; @hypermem]. Typed designs split memory by function, following cognitive accounts of episodic and semantic memory [@tulving1985memory; @episodic_memory_missing_piece]. MIRIX [@mirix] assigns six memory types to dedicated agents, and PersonaAgent [@zhang2026personaagent] pairs episodic and semantic memory for personalization. Memory stored in model parameters or latent states [@memoryllm; @mplus; @self-param; @behrouz2024titans] requires training and cannot be used with closed models. Evaluation has moved from recall over long conversations [@locomo; @longmemeval] to multi-session agentic tasks [@memoryagentbench; @he2026memoryarena], evolving environments [@ji2026clawarena], lifelong personalization [@memorycd], memory audits [@ma2026memaudit], and comparisons of memory substrates [@harnessmemory]. MIRIX-2.0 combines the typed and the graph directions. Complete typed records remain the system of record, a provenance hypergraph serves only as their index, and every graph fact cites its source records.

Multi-agent systems divide a task among LLM agents that take different roles [@guo2024masurvey; @luo2025agentsurvey], from role-playing and conversable agents [@li2023camel; @wu2024autogen] to software teams and groups of experts [@hong2024metagpt; @qian2024chatdev; @chen2024agentverse]. Agents can debate [@du2024debate; @liang2024debate; @huang2026madiave], optimize their workflow [@zhuge2024gptswarm; @zhang2025aflow], or communicate through latent representations [@yang2026xbridge]. They also fail in new ways [@cemri2025mas; @huang2026believed], and one response keeps humans in the loop as collaborators [@zou2026hasurvey; @zou2025collabintel; @wu2026hasbench]. Memory in these systems mostly serves the collaboration, through shared pools, graphs of team trajectories, and sharing across users under access control [@memory_sharing; @zhang2025gmemory; @rezazadeh2025collabmemory]. MIRIX-2.0 applies the multi-agent design to memory itself. A Meta Memory Manager routes each input to the agents of the six memory types, and each agent writes only the records of its own type.

Self-reflection lets an agent improve from feedback on its own behavior without updating its weights [@shinn2023reflexion; @madaan2023selfrefine; @park2023generative; @zhao2025marco]. Experience-driven methods turn such feedback into reusable knowledge, including insights [@zhao2024expel], executable skills [@wang2024voyager], workflows [@wang2025awm], reasoning strategies [@ouyang2026reasoningbank], and evolving playbooks and case banks [@suzgun2025cheatsheet; @zhang2026ace; @fang2026memp; @zhou2025memento]. Skills can co-evolve with the policy [@xia2026skillrl; @xia2026metaclaw], be verified and optimized as artifacts [@zhang2026coevoskills; @ma2026skillneo], or form a hypergraph [@xu2026hyperskill], and agents, their harnesses, and their memory architectures can improve themselves [@xia2026agent0; @xia2026rrsi; @liu2026evolvemem; @gao2026selfevolving; @fang2025selfevolving]. Consolidation can also run offline, before queries arrive or while the user is inactive [@lin2025sleeptime; @fang2026lightmem; @xia2026metaclaw; @ye2026autodreamer]. Rewriting memory carries risks. Consolidated memories can become faulty [@zhang2026faultymemories], and a defective skill can contaminate the skills distilled after it [@shang2026backfires]. AutoDream also consolidates outside the write path, but it limits what consolidation may change. On semantic memory it merges only anchors that denote the same entity and never rewrites stored records. On procedural memory it keeps a judged outcome, and each experience a skill edit cites, only after checking the quoted text.

# Conclusion {#sec:conclusion}

We presented MIRIX-2.0, a multi-agent memory system that keeps complete typed records, indexes them with a provenance hypergraph under explicit budgets, and runs AutoDream outside the write path to merge duplicate anchors and refine skills. It leads the compared systems on four of the five QA benchmarks, two of them with AutoDream disabled, reaching 91.4% on LoCoMo and 72.6% on LongMemEval-S, and it has the highest value on all four metrics for cross-domain review summarization on MemoryCD. On LoCoMo, the hypergraph and AutoDream each add accuracy over flat memory. As procedural memory, its distilled skills lead the compared memory methods in mean success on ALFWorld and WebShop, and with GPT-5.2 they reach 49.2% on MetaClaw-Bench, against 44.0% reported for the MetaClaw skill library. With AutoDream disabled, MIRIX-2.0 answers each question in less time than HyperMem on all five timed benchmarks, and on WebShop its skills cost the fewest tokens among the memory methods. One store serves both uses, and the system runs with closed and open models without fine-tuning. The results suggest that a memory layer can be practical, accurate, and efficient at once when complete records remain the system of record, the graph is only a bounded index, and consolidation changes how evidence is connected rather than what evidence exists.

Our ablations add components in groups. They do not isolate n-ary frames, cited source rows, occurrence identities, answer-time verification, or the judge, distiller, and curator of procedural memory, so the explanations in [3](#sec:experiments){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:experiments"} remain interpretations. The user-memory results come from single runs, partly scored by an LLM judge, the agent results average three runs with large spreads, and some baselines are values reported by their authors.

# Ethics and Broader Impact Statement {#ethics-and-broader-impact-statement .unnumbered}

MIRIX-2.0 lets assistants and task agents carry what users told them, and what the agents learned, from one session to the next. Every graph fact cites the records it was extracted from, so an answer can be traced to stored evidence. It is intended for assistants that users knowingly choose to use and should not be used to build profiles of people without their consent. A user-centric memory accumulates personal and possibly sensitive information. MIRIX-2.0 keeps sensitive verbatim values in a Knowledge Vault that the hypergraph does not index, but this does not replace consent and access control. A deployment should let users view, correct, and delete their memories, confine each memory to its user [@rezazadeh2025collabmemory], and use model providers or local models that meet its privacy requirements, because stored content is sent to the model. Memory can also make mistakes persistent [@zhang2026faultymemories; @shang2026backfires], and content injected into a conversation can be stored and later retrieved as if it were trusted. A wrong merge changes only the index, and a procedural edit keeps a citation only after its quote is checked, but in high-stakes settings learned skills and merged identities should still be reviewed by a person and stored memories audited periodically [@ma2026memaudit]. All experiments use publicly released benchmarks under their licenses. We collected no new data and ran no study with human participants. MemoryCD is built from public Amazon product reviews, and we use it as released without attempting to identify the reviewers.

## Memory types, routing, and writing {#app:method-routing}

The Meta Memory Manager is the first call of the write path ([\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"}, line [\[alg:route\]](#alg:route){reference-type="ref" reference="alg:route"}). The router reads the input and selects the memory types it updates. Each selected writer then reads the current store $P_u^{t-1}$ and produces a record that line [\[alg:commit\]](#alg:commit){reference-type="ref" reference="alg:commit"} commits to the evidence plane before any graph work begins. Formally, each input $x_t$ is mapped to a subset of six specialized writers, $$\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
    \mathcal K_t&=R_\theta(x_t),\\
    m_t^{(k)}&=W_k(x_t,P_u^{t-1}),\quad k\in\mathcal K_t,
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:routing}
\end{equation}$$ and each writer owns the schema and update operations of one memory type. Both steps of [\[eq:routing\]](#eq:routing){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:routing"} are language-model calls, which is why the router and the writers carry the $\star$ mark in [\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"}. [2](#tab:memory-types){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="tab:memory-types"} lists the six types.

::: {#tab:memory-types}
  Memory            Primary role                         Hypergraph indexed
  ----------------- ------------------------------------ --------------------
  Core              persistent user and agent state      no
  Episodic          time-grounded occurrences            yes
  Semantic          durable facts and concepts           yes
  Procedural        executable workflows                 no
  Resource          documents and multimodal artifacts   no
  Knowledge Vault   sensitive verbatim values            no

  : The six memory types of MIRIX-2.0 and their roles.
:::

The separation is functional. Only episodic and semantic memory are indexed by the hypergraph, because they hold the event and relational evidence that cross-memory traversal needs. The other types keep their direct, type-specific retrieval paths. Core memory holds the persistent state of the user and the agent, procedural memory holds the skills that [2.5](#sec:autodream){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:autodream"} refines, resource memory holds documents and multimodal artifacts, and the knowledge vault holds sensitive verbatim values. Each of them is read through its own typed path rather than through the graph. In [\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"}, the test $k\in\{\mathrm{episodic},\mathrm{semantic}\}$ is the only step of the write path that depends on whether a record is graph-indexed. In [2](#fig:mirix-user-centric){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="fig:mirix-user-centric"}, both user turns are routed to the episodic and semantic writers.

Before a record reaches the graph extractor, the writer prompts of our runs ask the episodic and semantic writers to keep complete lists, names, counts, titles, locations, and dates in the record's details instead of replacing them with a topical gist. Episodic writers store the time an event happened rather than the time it was mentioned, and they store a planned event with its planned date.

The write to the vector database commits before graph construction begins (line [\[alg:commit\]](#alg:commit){reference-type="ref" reference="alg:commit"}). Graph extraction is a fail-soft indexing hook, so a graph failure is logged but never rolls back a stored memory. The evidence plane is thus the durable system of record rather than a side effect of graph construction.

## Anchor admission, canonicalization, and frame extraction {#app:method-graph}

This part details lines [\[alg:extract\]](#alg:extract){reference-type="ref" reference="alg:extract"} to [\[alg:upsert\]](#alg:upsert){reference-type="ref" reference="alg:upsert"} of [\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"}, which turn a committed episodic or semantic record into the anchors and frames of [2.3](#sec:hypergraph){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:hypergraph"}.

The key $\kappa(n)$ of an anchor name normalizes case, punctuation, articles and other function words, word order, and conservative singular and plural variants while keeping numbers. In the code it lowercases the name, replaces punctuation with spaces, drops a fixed list of eleven articles and function words, singularizes tokens of at least four letters, and sorts the remaining tokens, so that *Mobile App* and *Mobile Apps* share one key. The key is deliberately conservative. Numbers and dates are preserved, so *10 Gallons* and *20 Gallons* stay distinct, and only plural endings are normalized, so *Training* and *Trains* stay apart. A single admission gate rejects generic filing terms, such as *advice*, *details*, *information*, *plan*, and *tips*, and dialogue-role pseudo-entities such as *user* and *assistant*, and it keeps at most 16 anchors for an episodic record and 18 for a semantic one. The caps bound the anchor space of a user, and they are sized for the arguments of an assertion rather than for a bag of keywords, so that one frame with seven arguments does not exhaust them. The gate is the [Admit]{.smallcaps} call of [\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"}, and because it runs on the canonical key, one entry such as *user* also blocks *User*, *Users*, and *the user*.

For every extracted entity, approximate nearest-neighbor search retrieves up to four existing anchors of the user whose cosine similarity is at least $0.72$, and one language-model call per record makes a conservative same-entity decision for all of its entities. The vector index scores a pair by $(1+\cos)/2$, so the thresholds in the code are 0.86 here and 0.93 for AutoDream ([6.5](#app:method-autodream){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-autodream"}). The call is instructed not to merge different quantities, different days or months, different form or model codes, or related but distinct concepts. Canonicalization runs before the admission gate, which is the order $\textsc{Admit}(\textsc{Canon}^\star(\Phi))$ of [\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"}, so the gate judges canonical names rather than surface forms. The candidates come from the anchors the user already has, which is what keeps naming consistent as the graph grows and why we call the step registry-guided.

The extractor behind [\[eq:frame\]](#eq:frame){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:frame"} is instructed to keep dates, prices, counts, durations, and measurements as literals on the frame rather than as anchors, so that values such as 7, \$420, or three months stay attached to the assertion they qualify. A frame is materialized only if its arguments resolve to at least two distinct anchors of the user, which is the test $|A_f|\ge 2$ of [\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"}. When a deterministic audit finds that the extraction misses high-signal source details, such as quoted phrases, numbers, or concrete visual content, one bounded repair call may add frames that the source supports. The repair cannot remove anything the primary extraction produced. The audit compares the words of the rendered frames with the source text. It lists the quoted phrases and the numbers of the source that the frames do not cover, it treats a source with visual content as uncovered when the frames cover less than 45 percent of its content words, and it sends at most twelve missing items to the repair call. A missing date alone does not trigger a repair, because session dates recur in many records and are already preserved by provenance. A missing date is added to a call that a missing quote, number, or visual detail already requires. Repair is the $\textsc{Repair}^\star$ call of line [\[alg:extract\]](#alg:extract){reference-type="ref" reference="alg:extract"}, and the frames it adds pass through the same canonicalization, admission, and identity rules as the primary ones.

## Retrieval budgets, lanes, scores, and fusion {#app:method-retrieval}

This part details the [Read]{.smallcaps} procedure of [\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"}, whose steps [2.4](#sec:retrieval){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:retrieval"} introduces.

::: wraptable
R0.36

  Operator         $B_q^B$   $B_q^R$
  -------------- --------- ---------
  Single                 6         4
  Temporal               6         6
  Intersection           7         7
  List-union             7         9
  Count                  8        12
:::

The planner [\[eq:query-plan\]](#eq:query-plan){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:query-plan"} fixes two row budgets for every query, and [\[tab:lane-budgets\]](#tab:lane-budgets){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="tab:lane-budgets"} lists them by operator. The budgets grow with the operator, because counting and set construction need broader evidence than a single-value lookup. A single-value question needs the one row that holds the value, so its cited-row budget is the smallest, whereas a count has to see many members of the set it counts. The evidence a question inspects is therefore bounded by its operator and not by the length of the history.

Beyond the fifteen anchors that vector recall returns, a relation query adds up to sixteen anchors that the question names exactly before the union is scored by [\[eq:anchor-score\]](#eq:anchor-score){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:anchor-score"}. Person hubs receive a larger penalty unless a relation query names them explicitly. Only the exactly named anchors carry degree and predicate information, so the hub and predicate terms act on them alone. The two entry routes serve different questions. Vector recall finds the anchors whose names paraphrase the question, and exact lookup finds the anchors that a relation question names, so that the relation lane can start from the named participants rather than from their nearest paraphrases.

The lanes follow three rules beyond those of [2.4](#sec:retrieval){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:retrieval"}. In the base-recall lane, same-chunk support and immediate temporal neighbors are recomputed from the provenance stored in the vector database instead of being duplicated as graph bookkeeping edges. In the relation-citation lane, predicate filtering runs inside Neo4j before the cap of 240 frames, and when the question names a concrete object, only frames that contain it are kept. The base lane is the $\textsc{Rows}(A_q)$ term and the relation lane the $\textsc{Adj}(A_q,\mathcal P_q)$ term of [\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"}. Recomputing same-chunk and temporal neighbors from provenance keeps the graph free of edges that only restate what the evidence plane already records. Filtering by predicate inside the database before the cap means that an explicitly named hub with hundreds of incident frames does not trigger a full traversal, and the frames that survive the cap are selected by a deterministic order on the frame identifier, so which frames pass does not depend on the traversal order of the database.

Two details of [\[eq:fact-score\]](#eq:fact-score){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:fact-score"} complete [2.4](#sec:retrieval){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:retrieval"}. When a frame has no extracted event time, the state term $s_{\mathrm{state}}$ falls back at half weight to the time of the source record, so the frame can still compete on time. For intersection questions, a hard shared-object group is preserved before these soft refinements, so recency cannot replace evidence that satisfies only one side of the intersection. The role term reads the typed roles of a frame, and the state term reads the status literal that marks an event as planned or completed ([2.3](#sec:hypergraph){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:hypergraph"}), which is how planned events are penalized in count, list-union, and temporal questions.

Rows in each lane are reranked with a shallow vector-rank prior and the lexical coverage of the query. Fusion runs separately for episodic and semantic rows within the row limit of a request. Near-duplicate episodic paraphrases are collapsed only when they share a calendar date, so the same wording on two different dates stays separate. Cited rows are filled first because a graph fact without the row that supports it would leave the answer unverifiable, and the date condition on paraphrase collapse is the retrieval-side counterpart of the occurrence identity [\[eq:episodic-frame-id\]](#eq:episodic-frame-id){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:episodic-frame-id"}, which keeps undated repetitions apart at write time.

## Answer-time verification {#app:method-verification}

The [Answer]{.smallcaps} procedure of [\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"} belongs to the evaluation harness rather than to the memory server, and the rule-based check of [2.4](#sec:retrieval){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:retrieval"} never inspects a reference answer. The check fires when it finds explicit uncertainty, a count without a number or one that disagrees with a listed count, a temporal answer without a time, an answer number or month that is absent from the evidence ledger and the question, ledger evidence with an incompatible role, or a planned event in a count question. It also fires on every intersection question. The extra search uses a query derived from $\Pi(q)$, or the question itself for a relation query. Most triggers mark a way in which a draft falls short of the evidence it was given or disagrees with it. A number or month that appears in neither the ledger nor the question was not retrieved, a count that disagrees with a listed count contradicts the evidence, and a planned event in a count question would count something that has not happened. Intersection questions always trigger the check because they need evidence for both sides, and a first retrieval that satisfies one side can look complete. The rewrite is line [\[alg:retry\]](#alg:retry){reference-type="ref" reference="alg:retry"} of [\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"}, so the procedure adds at most one search and one model call to a question.

## AutoDream schedule, candidates, and procedural refinement {#app:method-autodream}

This part details the [SemanticDream]{.smallcaps} and [ProceduralDream]{.smallcaps} procedures of [\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"} (lines [\[alg:dream-pairs\]](#alg:dream-pairs){reference-type="ref" reference="alg:dream-pairs"} to [\[alg:procedural\]](#alg:procedural){reference-type="ref" reference="alg:procedural"}) and expands [2.5](#sec:autodream){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:autodream"}.

Semantic AutoDream is an explicit service call that never schedules itself. In our protocol, the ingestion driver invokes AutoDream after every five chunks and once after the final chunk. The chunk identifiers of each call resolve to the newly written semantic rows, which define the frontier $\Delta_u$ of [\[eq:dream-contract\]](#eq:dream-contract){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:dream-contract"}, and the eligible anchors backed by those rows are the sources $\mathcal A_\Delta$ of [\[eq:dream-candidates\]](#eq:dream-candidates){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:dream-candidates"}. An intermediate call therefore examines only the anchors whose semantic rows the batch just wrote, and the final call also considers the eligible anchors that no earlier call used as a source.

The identity-stable types $\mathcal T_{\mathrm{safe}}$ of [2.5](#sec:autodream){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:autodream"} are person, organization, location, object, and concept. Anchors of the types event, date, content, method, and *other* are excluded deliberately, because merging two of them can collapse distinct occurrences or temporal context even when their names are close in embedding space. Both endpoints of a candidate pair must be backed by semantic rows and share the same eligible type, and the cosine threshold $0.86$ of [\[eq:dream-candidates\]](#eq:dream-candidates){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:dream-candidates"} is the index score $0.93$ under the $(1+\cos)/2$ scale of [6.2](#app:method-graph){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-graph"}.

An intermediate Dream verifies at most 80 unseen pairs from the current semantic delta. The final Dream considers the delta together with eligible anchors that have never served as a Dream source, and it verifies at most 120 pairs. If that round merges anything, one repair round examines only the touched representatives and verifies at most 60 more pairs. Explicit negative decisions are cached by anchor type, so later cycles do not pay again to reconsider the same pair. The cache key hashes the case-folded pair in sorted order together with the type, so the order in which a pair is encountered does not matter. Only explicit rejections are cached. A pair that a round did not reach within its cap is not recorded as rejected, so it becomes a candidate again whenever one of its anchors is in a later frontier. The intermediate rounds keep the graph consolidated while ingestion proceeds, and the final round sweeps up anchors that earlier rounds never used as a source. In [\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"}, the cache is the set $\mathcal N$ that line [\[alg:dream-pairs\]](#alg:dream-pairs){reference-type="ref" reference="alg:dream-pairs"} subtracts from the candidate pairs, and the per-round caps bound the number of $\textsc{Same}^\star$ calls the loop makes.

Two rules of the procedural path complete [2.5](#sec:autodream){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:autodream"}. The distiller judges the outcome of each pending session from quotes in its transcript, and an outcome whose quotes do not occur in the transcript is downgraded to unclear rather than trusted. Besides creating, editing, or deleting a skill, the curator can mark an experience as already covered, not worth a skill, or deferred, so it can decline an experience without changing the store. The quote check on outcomes and the quote check on citations are the procedural counterpart of the provenance that the hypergraph keeps for facts. They keep the skill library traceable although both $\textsc{Distill}^\star$ and $\textsc{Curate}^\star$ in [\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"} are language-model calls.

## Invariants, learned decisions, and complexity {#app:method-invariants}

[]{#app:method-complexity label="app:method-complexity"}

Four properties hold regardless of model behavior.

1.  Every returned graph fact exposes the identifiers of its source memories.

2.  Undated episodic occurrences from different memory rows receive different frame identities.

3.  Retrieval and Dream both run under explicit candidate and output caps.

4.  A failed graph write cannot destroy a committed memory, and a graph read that raises an error falls back to direct evidence retrieval.

Together with [\[eq:dream-contract\]](#eq:dream-contract){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="eq:dream-contract"}, the first and fourth invariants give the source fidelity required in [2.1](#sec:method-setup){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="sec:method-setup"}, the third gives bounded access, and the second gives temporal identity.

Language models route inputs, write typed memories, extract frames, repair missing source details, judge semantic identity, and distill and curate procedural experience. Together with answer generation, these are the calls marked $\star$ in [\[alg:mirix\]](#alg:mirix){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="alg:mirix"}. Deterministic code owns the safety-critical boundaries, namely admissible anchor names, candidate allowlists, frame identities, the representation of events and their status, provenance attachment, query operators, lane budgets, merge algebra, and mutation order. An LLM may propose that two anchors are identical, for instance, but it cannot name an anchor outside the retrieved candidate set or delete evidence directly.

Let $d$ be the number of admitted anchors in one memory, $h$ the number of frames extracted from it, $K_A$ the anchor-retrieval cap, and $K_F$ the adjacent-frame cap. Ignoring model inference and indexed database lookups, writing a memory into the graph costs $O(d+h)$. Post-retrieval ranking and fusion cost $O(K_A+K_F+K_M)$, where $K_M$ is the operator-dependent cited-row budget, and the system never materializes an unbounded evidence set from the vector database. For a Dream frontier of size $|\Delta_u|$, nearest-neighbor generation costs $O(|\Delta_u|\log|\mathcal A_u|)$ under the vector index, and LLM verification is capped at 80 pairs per intermediate cycle and 180 pairs across the two possible final rounds. The constants behind these symbols are the ones of this appendix. The admission gate bounds $d$ by 16 for an episodic record and 18 for a semantic one ([6.2](#app:method-graph){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-graph"}), $K_A$ is the fifteen anchors kept after scoring, $K_F$ is the cap of 240 frames, and $K_M$ is $B_q^R$, at most 12 ([6.3](#app:method-retrieval){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-retrieval"}). The 180 final pairs are the 120 of the final round plus the 60 of its repair round ([6.5](#app:method-autodream){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="app:method-autodream"}).

## Implementation and an example hypergraph {#app:method-implementation}

Evidence rows are stored in a vector database and the hypergraph in Neo4j. [5](#fig:graph-conv26){reference-type="ref+Label" reference="fig:graph-conv26"} shows part of the hypergraph that MIRIX-2.0 builds for LoCoMo conversation conv-26, in which Caroline and Melanie talk from May to October 2023. The figure keeps the 32 anchors that appear in the most frames, leaving out generic duplicates such as Family and kids, together with the 448 frames that join at least two of them.

The two speakers are the largest hubs, with 650 and 529 frames. The 94 frames that join only the two speakers lie between the hubs and are drawn in gray, and painting and art are reached from both sides. Each frame also keeps role labels, literals such as dates, and citations to its source records, which the figure leaves out. Through these frames one anchor gathers evidence across sessions. Self-Acceptance appears in 13 frames from 10 sessions, which link it to the LGBTQ support group, the pride parade, *Becoming Nicole*, and Caroline's painting *Embracing Identity*.
